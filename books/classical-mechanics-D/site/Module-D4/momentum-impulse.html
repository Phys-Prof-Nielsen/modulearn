D2.1 Linear Momentum and Impulse#

D2.1.1 Motivation#

In introductory physics, we often get very comfortable with Newton’s second law written in its simplest form: force equals mass times acceleration. This works well for many everyday problems — pushing a block, a ball rolling down a ramp, or an object in free fall. But as our understanding deepens, it becomes clear that this familiar expression is really only a special case. It assumes that the system’s mass never changes, and that we can neatly describe the motion by tracking acceleration. Real physical systems are often more complicated.

Think about a baseball being struck by a bat. The force is enormous but it only lasts for a few thousandths of a second. It would be nearly impossible to calculate the acceleration of the ball during that brief, violent interaction. Yet, the change in the ball’s momentum is easy to measure, and that is all we really need to know. Or consider a freight car being filled with sand while it rolls along a track. The car’s mass is steadily increasing as sand pours in, which means that “force equals mass times acceleration” is no longer a reliable guide. Even more striking is the case of rockets, where the very act of motion depends on shedding mass as exhaust. Here, the traditional way of writing Newton’s law simply breaks down.

In all of these cases, the impulse–momentum theorem becomes the more natural and more powerful principle. It tells us that what really matters is the relationship between the net external force acting over time and the resulting change in momentum of the system. Instead of struggling to calculate accelerations or worrying about changing masses, we can focus directly on how momentum is transferred. This perspective is not just mathematically cleaner — it is physically deeper. Momentum is a conserved quantity in nature, and conservation laws often provide a much more reliable foundation for analysis than trying to track forces instant by instant.

As we move forward into more advanced mechanics, we will see that the impulse–momentum theorem is not a “special tool for special problems,” but in fact the most general form of Newton’s second law. It applies equally well to collisions, to continuous mass flows, and to systems where the very definition of acceleration becomes awkward. Recognizing this shifts our thinking: instead of treating momentum as a secondary idea derived from force and acceleration, we begin to see it as the central principle that unifies all of mechanics. This shift is what will allow us to handle variable-mass systems in the next phase, and it is also what connects directly to the language of modern physics, where momentum and conservation laws play a far more fundamental role than acceleration ever does.

D2.1.2 Definition - Momentum#

Momentum is arguable the most fundamental concept in Newtonian mechanics. Newton’s laws of motion talks about motion and change in motion, but does not specify what motion is.

Linear momentum is linear motion.

We define the classical linear momentum of a particle with mass \(m\) and moving with a velocity \(\vec{v}\) as

\[\vec{p} = m\vec{v} \]

Similarly, we define the center of mass linear momentum of an extended systme/object as

\[\vec{P} = M\vec{V} \]

where \(M\) is the mass of the system and \(\vec{V}\) is the velocity of the center of mass.

D2.1.3 Definition - Impulse#

When a particle is subject to a force acting through some elapsed time, the force will transfer momentum to/from the particle depending on the interaction. The momentum transfer is also known as impulse and is defined as

\[\vec{i} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\vec{f}dt \]

Similarly, we define the center of mass impulse of an extended systme/object as

\[\vec{I} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\vec{F}dt \]

Notice that the definite integral implies a change in something, while the left side of the equations do not explicitly show a change. One should remember that the impulse is a transfer of momentum and therefore do represent changes.

D2.1.4 Momentum-Impulse Theorem: Newton’s 2nd Law#

Newton defined his 2nd law as: “A particle’s motion will change upon a net interaction through some time, and it is quantified in differential form as

\[ d\vec{p} = \vec{f}_{net}dt \tag{1}\]

We can then readily observe that the net impulse is the same as the particle’s change in momentum in integral from:

\[\Delta\vec{p} = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\vec{f}_{net}dt \tag{2}\]

The momentum-impulse theorem is often stated in either form (1) or (2).

In derivative form, we have the more common form of Newton’s 2nd law:

\[ \vec{f} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt} \]
Box 1

A particle of mass 2.5 kg is moving in the horizontal \(xy\)-plane with a speed of 45 m/s when hits a fixed wall at an angle of 65\(^\circ\) with respect to the normal of the wall. The particle rebounds at an angle of 50.0\(^\circ\) with respect to the normal and a speed of 20.0 m/s. The contact time between the particle and the wall is 0.010 s. Let x-direction be perpendicular to and away from the wall and the y-direction parallel to the wall in the direction of motion.

  1. What is the impulse due to the impact force?

  2. What are the components of the impact force?

  3. What can you say about conservation of momentum and energy?

%reset -f
import numpy as np

m = 2.5
v1 = 45
theta1 = np.radians(65)
v2 = 20.0
theta2 = np.radians(50.0)
dt = 0.01

v1x = -v1*np.cos(theta1)
v1y = v1*np.sin(theta1)
v2x = v2*np.cos(theta2)
v2y = v2*np.sin(theta2)

v1 = np.array([v1x, v1y])
v2 = np.array([v2x, v2y])

p1 = m*v1
p2 = m*v2

i = p2 - p1
print('The impulse experienced by the particle is: '+str(i)+' kgm/s')

f = i/dt
print('The force components acting on the particle are: '+str(f)+' N')
