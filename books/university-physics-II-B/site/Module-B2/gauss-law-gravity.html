B2.2 Gauss’s Law for Gravity#

B2.2.1 Motivation#

We have four fundamental forces in physics, each arising from fundamental interactions:

  • Gravity

  • Electromagnetic

  • Strong nuclear

  • Weak nuclear

Every known force we encounter can be explained in terms of these four interactions, all of which emerge from what we understand as field interactions. In this sense, fields are more fundamental than forces themselves.

So far, we have primarily discussed the gravitational field in terms of Newton’s law of universal gravitation, which describes the interaction between masses. However, in more advanced physics, the focus shifts from forces to the properties and behavior of the fields themselves. One key concept that arises in this context is flux—a measure of how much of a field passes through a given surface.

The flux concept is central to Gauss’s Law, which exists for gravitational, electric, and magnetic fields. Since we are already somewhat familiar with Newtonian gravity, we will introduce the idea of flux in the context of the gravitational field before exploring its deeper implications. Gauss’s Law for gravity provides a powerful alternative perspective on Newton’s law, revealing an elegant mathematical structure that simplifies many problems in gravitational physics.

B2.2.2 Gravitational Field Flux#

In general, the flux is defined in terms of a vector field passing through a surface per unit time. We can say that the flux is a measure of how much of a field quantity is passing through a given surface.

\[ \Phi = \int \vec{g}\cdot d\vec{A} \tag{1} \]

B2.2.3 Gauss’s Law#

We have previously labeled the acceleration of gravity at the Earth’s surface as \(g = 9.81\) m/s\(^2\). However, we know that it changes as we move away from Earth. The objective of these notes is to learn how to calculate the gravitational field (acceleration) as we move away from the Earth’s surface as well as also from certain non-spherical mass distributions.

The tool to perform these calculations is Gauss’s law for gravity:

Gauss’s Law

\[ \oint \vec{g}\cdot d\vec{A} = -4\pi Gm_{\textrm{enclosed}} \tag{2}\]

where \(\vec{g}\) is the gravitational field, \(d\vec{A}\) is an area element with a direction pointing out of the closed surface, known as the Gaussian surface, that we are integrating over, \(G\) is a constant known as the universal constant of gravity, and \(m_{enclosed}\) is the mass that is inside the closed surface. We can understand the \(m_{enclosed}\) to be the source of the gravitational field.

We can re-write the integral in terms of the vector magnitudes using the definition of the dot product:

\[ \oint g\cos\theta dA = -4\pi Gm_{\textrm{enclosed}} \tag{3}\]

where \(\theta\) is the angle between the gravitational field and the area vector (unit vector pointing out of the closed surface).

For a spherical symmetric source such as the Earth (the blue planet in the Figure below), the obvious surface to integrate over is a sphere. Any point on the sphere is the same distance away from the source and the field strength is therefore the same at any point on the sphere (\(g = constant\)). Furthermore, since the normal area element \(d\vec{A}\) is pointing away from the sphere, the angle between \(\vec{g}\) and \(d\vec{A}\) is \(180^\circ\).

Box 1

Consider the Earth to be a perfect sphere. Imagine we are enclosing a closed spherical surface encapsulating the Earth. Using equation 1, show that the gravitational field at the surface of the Earth or outside the Earth is

\[ g = \frac{GM_{Earth}}{r^2} \]

where \(M_{Earth}\) is the mass of the Earth (the mass inside the imaginary Gaussian sphere) and \(r\) is the distance away from the center of the Earth (the radius of the Gaussian sphere) with the condition that \(r \geq R_{Earth}\). The surface area of a sphere with radius \(r\) is \(A_{sphere} = 4\pi r^2\).
