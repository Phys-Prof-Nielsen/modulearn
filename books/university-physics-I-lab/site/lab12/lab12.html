Lab 12: Simple Harmonic Motion with a Phone Pendulum#

Overview#

In this lab we will investigate simple harmonic motion (SHM) using a pendulum made from a phone suspended by a string. We will use the Phyphox pendulum tool to measure the oscillation period and angular frequency directly from the motion of the phone.

By measuring the effective pendulum length from the pivot point to the approximate center of mass of the phone, we can predict the angular frequency theoretically and compare it to the measured value.

This lab connects experimental motion data to the mathematical model of SHM and helps us test how well a real pendulum follows the small-angle approximation.

Learning Objectives#

By the end of this lab, we will be able to:

  1. Model a pendulum as an example of simple harmonic motion for small oscillations.

  2. Measure the effective pendulum length from the pivot to the phone’s approximate center of mass.

  3. Use theory to calculate the expected angular frequency

\[ \omega_{\text{theory}} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}} \]

  1. Use Phyphox to measure the pendulum’s oscillation period and angular frequency.

  2. Compare theoretical and experimental values and discuss sources of disagreement.

  3. Evaluate the assumptions behind the small-angle pendulum model.

Background Theory#

1. Simple Harmonic Motion#

A system is in simple harmonic motion when the restoring influence is proportional to displacement and directed toward equilibrium.

A common mathematical model for SHM is

\[ x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi) \]

where:

  • \(A\) is the amplitude

  • \(\omega\) is the angular frequency

  • \(\phi\) is the phase constant

The angular frequency is related to the period \(T\) by

\[ \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} \]

and the ordinary frequency is

\[ f = \frac{1}{T} \]

so that

\[ \omega = 2\pi f \]

2. Pendulum Motion#

A simple pendulum consists of a mass suspended from a pivot by a light string. When the pendulum is displaced slightly and released, it oscillates back and forth.

For small angles, the pendulum behaves approximately like an SHM system. Its angular frequency is

\[ \omega_{\text{theory}} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}} \]

where:

  • \(g = 9.80\ \text{m/s}^2\) is the acceleration due to gravity

  • \(L\) is the effective length of the pendulum

The period is therefore

\[ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} \]

3. Effective Length of the Phone Pendulum#

In an ideal pendulum, the entire mass is concentrated at a single point. For a real phone pendulum, the mass is distributed across the phone. Therefore, the effective pendulum length should be measured from the pivot point to the approximate center of mass of the phone.

If the string length is \(L_s\) and the distance from the top attachment point on the phone to the phone’s center of mass is estimated as \(d\), then the effective pendulum length is

\[ L = L_s + d \]

In many cases, a reasonable estimate for the center of mass is near the geometric center of the phone.

4. Percent Difference#

To compare theory and experiment, we will calculate the percent difference between the measured and predicted angular frequencies:

\[ \%\text{ difference} = \frac{|\omega_{\text{measured}} - \omega_{\text{theory}}|}{\omega_{\text{theory}}}\times 100\% \]

Equipment#

  • Smartphone

  • String or cord

  • Tape, clamp, or secure attachment method

  • Meter stick or measuring tape

  • Phyphox app installed on the phone

  • Support stand or other stable hanging point

Experimental Setup#

Attach the phone securely to a string so that it can swing like a pendulum. The setup should allow the phone to oscillate freely in a vertical plane.

Make sure:

  • the phone is securely attached

  • the pivot point is stable

  • the oscillation angle remains small

  • the phone will not hit nearby objects during motion

Use the Pendulum experiment in Phyphox to record the motion.

Procedure#

Part A — Measure the Pendulum Geometry#

  1. Measure the length of the string from the pivot point to the point where the string attaches to the phone. Record this as \(L_s\).

  2. Measure the dimensions of the phone and estimate the location of its center of mass.

  3. Estimate the distance from the string attachment point on the phone to the center of mass. Record this as \(d\).

  4. Compute the effective pendulum length:

\[ L = L_s + d \]

  1. Record all measurements with units and reasonable uncertainty estimates.

Part B — Predict the Angular Frequency#

Using your measured effective length, calculate the theoretical angular frequency:

\[ \omega_{\text{theory}} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}} \]

Then calculate the theoretical period:

\[ T_{\text{theory}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} \]

Record both values.

Part C — Measure the Motion with Phyphox#

  1. Open the Phyphox app and select the pendulum experiment.

  2. Set the pendulum swinging with a small initial angle. Try to keep the release gentle so that the phone swings in a single plane.

  3. Start the measurement and allow the pendulum to oscillate for many cycles.

  4. Record the measured period \(T_{\text{measured}}\) from Phyphox.

  5. Use the measured period to compute the experimental angular frequency:

\[ \omega_{\text{measured}} = \frac{2\pi}{T_{\text{measured}}} \]

  1. If Phyphox reports frequency directly, you may also use

\[ \omega_{\text{measured}} = 2\pi f \]

  1. Repeat the measurement at least three times and average your results.

Part D — Compare Theory and Experiment#

  1. Compare your theoretical and measured values of angular frequency.

  2. Calculate the percent difference:

\[ \%\text{ difference} = \frac{|\omega_{\text{measured}} - \omega_{\text{theory}}|}{\omega_{\text{theory}}}\times 100\% \]

  1. Decide whether your results agree reasonably well.

Part E — Optional Extension#

Repeat the experiment for a different pendulum length.

  • Does a longer pendulum produce a larger or smaller angular frequency?

  • Does the trend agree with

\[ \omega \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{L}} \, ? \]

If time permits, test whether larger initial angles change the measured period noticeably.

Data Analysis#

For your analysis, show the following clearly:

  1. Your measured values of \(L_s\) and \(d\)

  2. Your calculation of the effective length \(L\)

  3. Your theoretical calculation of \(\omega_{\text{theory}}\)

  4. Your measured average period \(T_{\text{measured}}\)

  5. Your calculation of \(\omega_{\text{measured}}\)

  6. Your percent difference between theory and experiment

A sample structure is:

\[ L = L_s + d \]
\[ \omega_{\text{theory}} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}} \]
\[ \omega_{\text{measured}} = \frac{2\pi}{T_{\text{measured}}} \]
\[ \%\text{ difference} = \frac{|\omega_{\text{measured}} - \omega_{\text{theory}}|}{\omega_{\text{theory}}}\times 100\% \]

Discussion Questions#

Answer the following in complete sentences:

  1. Why must the pendulum angle remain small for the SHM model to work well?

  2. Why is the effective length measured to the phone’s center of mass rather than just to the top of the phone?

  3. Did your measured angular frequency agree with theory? If not, what are the most likely sources of error?

  4. How would increasing the pendulum length affect the period?

  5. Would this setup still behave like an ideal simple pendulum for very large swing angles? Why or why not?

  6. How might air resistance, shifting motion of the phone, or an inaccurate center-of-mass estimate affect the result?

Sources of Uncertainty#

Some common uncertainties in this lab include:

  • uncertainty in locating the phone’s center of mass

  • slight motion outside a single plane

  • large release angle

  • reaction or sensor limitations

  • stretch in the string

  • imperfect pivot point

  • damping from air resistance

Be specific about which of these mattered most in your experiment.

Conclusion#

In this lab we modeled a phone pendulum as a simple harmonic oscillator. By estimating the distance from the pivot to the phone’s center of mass, we calculated a theoretical angular frequency using

\[ \omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}} \]

We then used Phyphox to measure the pendulum’s actual oscillation and compared experiment to theory. The quality of agreement shows how well a real pendulum follows the ideal SHM model and highlights the importance of small-angle motion and careful length measurement.

What to Turn In#

  1. Completed data tables

  2. Theoretical calculation of \(\omega_{\text{theory}}\) and \(T_{\text{theory}}\)

  3. Experimental determination of \(T_{\text{measured}}\) and \(\omega_{\text{measured}}\)

  4. Percent difference calculation

  5. Answers to the discussion questions

  6. A short conclusion summarizing whether theory and experiment agreed
