Lab 3 — Terminal Voltage vs Load Current: Experimental Determination of Internal Resistance#

Overview#

Real power sources (batteries and power supplies) are not ideal. When a current is drawn, the terminal voltage decreases due to the source’s internal resistance.

In this lab you will:

  • model a real source as an ideal emf \(\mathcal{E}\) in series with an internal resistance \(r\),

  • measure the terminal voltage and current for different load resistors,

  • use a linear fit to determine the internal resistance of the source,

  • apply Ohm’s law in a physically meaningful experimental context.

Only one load resistor at a time is used. No series or parallel resistor combinations are required.

Learning Objectives#

By the end of this lab, you should be able to:

  1. Explain why terminal voltage decreases as load current increases.

  2. Apply Ohm’s law to a circuit containing a real (non-ideal) voltage source.

  3. Experimentally determine the internal resistance of a source.

  4. Interpret the slope and intercept of a \(V\) vs. \(I\) graph.

  5. Distinguish between emf and terminal voltage.

Background Theory#

Real Source Model#

A real voltage source can be modeled as an ideal emf \(\mathcal{E}\) in series with an internal resistance \(r\).

When a load resistor \(R\) is connected, the circuit current is

\[ I = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R + r}. \]

The terminal voltage across the load resistor is

\[ V = IR. \]

Substituting for \(I\) gives

\[ V = \mathcal{E} - Ir. \]

This predicts a linear relationship between terminal voltage and current:

\[ V = \mathcal{E} - rI. \]

From a linear fit to \(V\) vs. \(I\):

  • the vertical intercept is \(\mathcal{E}\),

  • the slope is \(-r\).

Equipment#

  • DC power source (battery pack)

  • Digital multimeter

  • Set of single resistors (e.g. 22 \(\Omega\), 47 \(\Omega\), 100 \(\Omega\), 220 \(\Omega\), 470 \(\Omega\))

  • Connecting leads or breadboard

Experimental Procedure#

Part 1 — Open-Circuit Voltage#

  1. Do not connect any load resistor to the source.

  2. Measure the voltage across the source terminals.

  3. Record this value as the open-circuit voltage \(V_{\text{OC}}\).

Part 2 — Load Measurements#

For each load resistor \(R\):

  1. Connect the resistor to the source.

  2. Measure the current \(I\) through the circuit.

  3. Measure the terminal voltage \(V\) across the load resistor.

  4. Record both values in your data table.

  5. Disconnect the resistor before switching to the next value.

Use at least five different load resistors.

Data Table#

Create a table with the following columns:

Trial

Load Resistance \(R\) (\(\Omega\))

Terminal Voltage \(V\) (V)

Current \(I\) (A)

Data Analysis#

Graphical Analysis#

  1. Plot terminal voltage \(V\) on the vertical axis.

  2. Plot current \(I\) on the horizontal axis.

  3. Fit the data to a straight line of the form

\[ V = a + bI. \]

Interpretation#

From the fit parameters:

\[ \mathcal{E} = a, \]
\[ r = -b. \]

Report both values with appropriate units.

Error Analysis#

All experimental measurements have uncertainty. In this lab, uncertainty enters primarily through measurements of voltage and current, and it affects your determination of the emf \(\mathcal{E}\) and internal resistance \(r\).

Measurement Uncertainty#

For each measured quantity, record the uncertainty based on the digital multimeter specifications or display resolution:

  • Voltage uncertainty: \(\delta V\)

  • Current uncertainty: \(\delta I\)

If no manufacturer specifications are provided, you may estimate uncertainty as one least significant digit of the meter display.

Uncertainty in the \(V\) vs. \(I\) Graph#

You will determine \(\mathcal{E}\) and \(r\) from a linear fit to the data

\[ V = a + bI. \]

The fitting routine provides:

  • best-fit values of \(a\) and \(b\),

  • uncertainties \(\delta a\) and \(\delta b\).

Details on how the uncertainties in the fit parameters are calculated are provided in the Appendix on Manual Uncertainty Calculations at the end of this note.

These uncertainties represent how well your data constrain the model.

From the fit:

\[ \mathcal{E} = a \pm \delta a, \]
\[ r = -b \pm \delta b. \]

Be sure to report uncertainties with appropriate significant figures.

Interpretation of Uncertainty#

In your analysis, comment on:

  • whether the uncertainty in \(r\) is dominated by low-current or high-current data,

  • how the spread of your data points affects the slope determination,

  • whether your uncertainty in \(\mathcal{E}\) is consistent with the measured open-circuit voltage \(V_{\text{OC}}\).

Common Sources of Experimental Uncertainty#

Discuss at least two of the following in your report:

  • finite resolution of the multimeter,

  • fluctuations in battery voltage during the experiment,

  • contact resistance at leads and connections,

  • heating of the load resistor at higher currents,

  • small variations in internal resistance as current changes.

Analysis Questions#

Answer in complete sentences.

  1. Why does the terminal voltage decrease as the current increases?

  2. What physical property of the source does the slope of the \(V\) vs. \(I\) graph represent?

  3. Compare your fitted value of \(\mathcal{E}\) with the open-circuit voltage \(V_{\text{OC}}\). Are they exactly the same? Why or why not?

  4. Which data points (low current or high current) most strongly affect the determination of \(r\)? Explain.

  5. What would the \(V\) vs. \(I\) graph look like if the internal resistance were zero?

Discussion#

In your discussion, address:

  • the physical meaning of internal resistance,

  • how your data demonstrate that the source is non-ideal,

  • major sources of uncertainty in this experiment,

  • one real-world implication of internal resistance (for example, voltage drop in batteries under heavy load).

Report Requirements#

In addition to the required format, ,ake sure your submitted report include:

  1. A clearly labeled \(V\) vs. \(I\) graph with a best-fit line

  2. Calculated values of \(\mathcal{E}\) and \(r\)

  3. Answers to all analysis questions

  4. Include the discussion points.

Notes and Tips#

  • Use consistent units throughout your analysis.

  • Allow the source to rest briefly between measurements if it warms up.

  • A linear \(V\)–\(I\) relationship is strong evidence that the source behaves like an emf in series with an internal resistance.

Appendix — Manual Calculation of Fit Parameter Uncertainties#

This appendix shows how the uncertainties in the intercept and slope of a linear fit can be calculated directly from the data using standard least-squares methods. You are not required to perform these calculations by hand for this lab, but the results are equivalent to the uncertainties reported by Python or Excel fitting routines.

Linear Model#

The data are modeled by a straight line

\[ V = a + bI, \]

where \(a\) is the intercept and \(b\) is the slope.

Residuals and Data Scatter#

After determining the best-fit values of \(a\) and \(b\), define the residuals

\[ \Delta V_i = V_i - (a + b I_i). \]

The variance of the data about the fit is

\[ \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{N - 2} \sum_i (\Delta V_i)^2, \]

where \(N\) is the number of data points and the factor \((N-2)\) accounts for the two fitted parameters.

Uncertainty in the Slope#

The uncertainty in the slope \(b\) is

\[ \delta b = \sqrt{ \frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_i (I_i - \bar I)^2} }, \]

where \(\bar I\) is the mean of the current values.

Uncertainty in the Intercept#

The uncertainty in the intercept \(a\) is

\[ \delta a = \sqrt{ \sigma^2 \left( \frac{1}{N} + \frac{\bar I^2}{\sum_i (I_i - \bar I)^2} \right) }. \]

Interpretation#

These expressions show that:

  • the uncertainty in the slope (and therefore in the internal resistance \(r = -b\)) decreases when the current values are spread over a wide range,

  • clustering data points near a single current value leads to a poorly determined slope,

  • the uncertainties reported by software fitting routines come directly from these formulas.

Practical Note#

In this lab, you should obtain \(\delta a\) and \(\delta b\) from the fitting routine used in Python or Excel. The purpose of this appendix is to demonstrate that these uncertainties have a clear mathematical and physical origin and are not arbitrary outputs of the software.
