B3.6 The Spring Pendulum: A Capstone Project in Nonlinear Dynamics#

B3.6.1 Motivation#

The spring pendulum is one of the richest systems in classical mechanics. It consists of a mass attached to a spring, where the mass is free both to stretch the spring and to swing like a pendulum.

At first glance, this system appears simple. It is just a mass, a spring, and gravity. However, its motion can exhibit an extraordinary range of behavior:

  • nearly independent spring and pendulum motion,

  • energy exchange between radial and angular motion,

  • internal resonance,

  • autoparametric resonance,

  • phase locking between modes,

  • complicated trajectories in the \((x,y)\) plane,

  • Poincaré sections,

  • and chaotic motion.

In this capstone project, we will use the spring pendulum as a miniature laboratory for nonlinear dynamics. The goal is not simply to solve one problem, but to investigate a system the way a physicist would: by deriving a model, simulating the equations, visualizing the motion, and interpreting the results.

Spring Pendulum

A spring pendulum is a system in which a mass attached to a spring can move both radially and angularly.

The radial coordinate describes stretching of the spring, while the angular coordinate describes swinging motion.

Because these two motions are coupled, the system can exhibit nonlinear energy exchange, resonance, and chaos.

This project brings together many ideas developed earlier in the module:

  • Lagrangian mechanics,
  • coupled oscillators,
  • normal modes,
  • parametric resonance,
  • nonlinear oscillations,
  • phase portraits,
  • Poincaré sections,
  • and chaos.

A particularly important feature of this system is that the motion can be visualized directly in physical space. If the spring length is

\[ r(t), \]

and the angle from the vertical is

\[ \theta(t), \]

then the Cartesian coordinates of the mass are

\[ x(t)=r(t)\sin\theta(t), \]

and

\[ y(t)=-r(t)\cos\theta(t). \]

The resulting \((x,y)\) trajectory often reveals beautiful geometric patterns, ranging from nearly regular curves to complicated irregular motion.

Capstone Perspective

The spring pendulum is not merely another example. It is a system where many of the central ideas of nonlinear dynamics appear naturally in one physical model.

By the end of this project, we will have used analytical mechanics, numerical methods, and visualization to understand how a deceptively simple mechanical system can produce remarkably complex motion.

In the next section, we will build the model by choosing generalized coordinates and deriving the Lagrangian.

B3.6.2 Building the Model#

To analyze the spring pendulum, we must first choose a set of generalized coordinates that completely specify the position of the mass.

Because the mass can both stretch the spring and swing about the pivot, two coordinates are required. Our goal is to derive the Lagrangian

\[ L=T-U \]

for this system.

Box Activity — Constructing the Lagrangian of a Spring Pendulum

Consider a mass

\[ m = 0.50~\mathrm{kg} \]

attached to a spring having spring constant

\[ k = 20~\mathrm{N/m} \]

and unstretched length

\[ \ell_0 = 0.30~\mathrm{m}. \]

Use the generalized coordinates

\[ r \qquad\text{and}\qquad \theta, \]

where

  • \(r\) is the instantaneous spring length,

  • \(\theta\) is measured from the downward vertical.

Work through the following steps.

  1. Determine the kinetic energy

\[ T. \]

  1. Determine the elastic potential energy stored in the spring.

  2. Determine the gravitational potential energy.

  3. Construct the total potential energy

\[ U. \]

  1. Obtain the Lagrangian

\[ L=T-U. \]
Activity solution

Since

\[ v^2 = \dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2, \]

the kinetic energy is

\[ T = \frac12m \left( \dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2 \right). \]

The elastic potential energy of the spring is

\[ U_s = \frac12k(r-\ell_0)^2. \]

Because the angle is measured from the downward vertical, the gravitational potential energy is

\[ U_g = -mgr\cos\theta. \]

Therefore,

\[ U = \frac12k(r-\ell_0)^2 - mgr\cos\theta. \]

The Lagrangian becomes

\[ \boxed{ L = \frac12m \left( \dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2 \right) - \frac12k(r-\ell_0)^2 + mgr\cos\theta } \]

This Lagrangian contains all the information needed to determine the motion of the spring pendulum.

In the next section, we will use this Lagrangian to derive the coupled nonlinear equations of motion.

B3.6.3 Equations of Motion#

Having constructed the Lagrangian, our next goal is to determine the equations governing the motion of the spring pendulum.

Because the coordinates

\[ r \qquad\text{and}\qquad \theta \]

are coupled, we expect to obtain two nonlinear differential equations. These equations form the mathematical model that will be used throughout the remainder of this project.

Box Activity — Deriving the Equations of Motion

Starting from the Lagrangian

\[ L = \frac12m \left( \dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2 \right) - \frac12k(r-\ell_0)^2 + mgr\cos\theta, \]

apply Lagrange’s equations

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_i} \right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i} = 0 \]

to each generalized coordinate.

Work through the following steps.

  1. Apply Lagrange’s equation to the coordinate

\[ r. \]

  1. Simplify the resulting expression to obtain the radial equation of motion.

  2. Apply Lagrange’s equation to the coordinate

\[ \theta. \]

  1. Simplify the resulting expression to obtain the angular equation of motion.

  2. Verify that the two equations are coupled.

Activity solution

For the radial coordinate,

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot r} = m\dot r, \]

so that

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot r} \right) = m\ddot r. \]

Furthermore,

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial r} = mr\dot\theta^2 -k(r-\ell_0) +mg\cos\theta. \]

Therefore,

\[ m\ddot r - mr\dot\theta^2 + k(r-\ell_0) - mg\cos\theta = 0, \]

or

\[ \boxed{ \ddot r = r\dot\theta^2 - \frac{k}{m}(r-\ell_0) + g\cos\theta } \]

For the angular coordinate,

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta} = mr^2\dot\theta, \]

and

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \left( mr^2\dot\theta \right) = mr^2\ddot\theta + 2mr\dot r\dot\theta. \]

Also,

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta} = -mgr\sin\theta. \]

Hence,

\[ mr^2\ddot\theta + 2mr\dot r\dot\theta + mgr\sin\theta = 0, \]

or

\[ \boxed{ \ddot\theta = -\frac{2\dot r\dot\theta}{r} - \frac{g}{r}\sin\theta } \]

These coupled nonlinear equations govern the motion of the spring pendulum.

To solve these equations numerically, it is convenient to rewrite them as a system of first-order differential equations.

Box Activity — Converting to a First-Order System

Introduce the state variables

\[ x_1=r, \qquad x_2=\dot r, \qquad x_3=\theta, \qquad x_4=\dot\theta. \]

  1. Express

\[ \dot x_1 \]

through

\[ \dot x_4 \]

in terms of

\[ x_1,x_2,x_3,x_4. \]

  1. Verify that the spring pendulum is described by four coupled first-order differential equations.

  2. Explain why this form is particularly useful for numerical methods such as Euler-Cromer and Runge-Kutta.

Activity solution

Defining

\[ x_1=r, \qquad x_2=\dot r, \qquad x_3=\theta, \qquad x_4=\dot\theta, \]

gives

\[ \boxed{ \dot x_1=x_2 } \]
\[ \boxed{ \dot x_2 = x_1x_4^2 - \frac{k}{m}(x_1-\ell_0) + g\cos x_3 } \]
\[ \boxed{ \dot x_3=x_4 } \]
\[ \boxed{ \dot x_4 = -\frac{2x_2x_4}{x_1} - \frac{g}{x_1}\sin x_3 } \]

These four first-order equations are ideally suited for numerical integration and will form the basis for all subsequent simulations in this capstone project.

B3.6.4 Numerical Solution and Motion in Physical Space#

The coupled equations derived in the previous section are too complicated to solve analytically. Fortunately, they are ideally suited for numerical integration.

Our strategy will be to:

  1. integrate the four first-order equations numerically,

  2. compute the Cartesian coordinates

\[ x=r\sin\theta, \]
\[ y=-r\cos\theta, \]

  1. visualize the motion of the mass in physical space.

The \((x,y)\) trajectory often reveals beautiful geometric patterns and provides an intuitive picture of the dynamics.

Consequently, the path traced by the mass may resemble a distorted ellipse, a flower-like pattern, or more complicated curves depending on the parameters and initial conditions.

This geometric viewpoint provides one of the most intuitive ways to understand the dynamics of the system.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Spring pendulum simulation
#
# State vector:
# y = [r, r_dot, theta, theta_dot]
# ------------------------------------------------------------

# Physical parameters
m = 0.50          # kg
k = 20.0          # N/m
L0 = 0.30         # m
g = 9.81          # m/s^2

# Time settings
dt = 0.001
t_max = 20
t = np.arange(0, t_max, dt)

# Initial conditions
r0 = 0.40
r_dot0 = 0.0
theta0 = np.radians(20.0)
theta_dot0 = 0.0

def derivatives(state):
    r, r_dot, theta, theta_dot = state

    r_ddot = (
        r*theta_dot**2
        - (k/m)*(r - L0)
        + g*np.cos(theta)
    )

    theta_ddot = (
        -2*r_dot*theta_dot/r
        - (g/r)*np.sin(theta)
    )

    return np.array([r_dot, r_ddot, theta_dot, theta_ddot])

def rk4_step(state, dt):
    k1 = derivatives(state)
    k2 = derivatives(state + 0.5*dt*k1)
    k3 = derivatives(state + 0.5*dt*k2)
    k4 = derivatives(state + dt*k3)

    return state + (dt/6)*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

# Arrays
r = np.zeros(len(t))
r_dot = np.zeros(len(t))
theta = np.zeros(len(t))
theta_dot = np.zeros(len(t))

# Initial state
state = np.array([r0, r_dot0, theta0, theta_dot0])

# Numerical integration
for i in range(len(t)):
    r[i] = state[0]
    r_dot[i] = state[1]
    theta[i] = state[2]
    theta_dot[i] = state[3]

    state = rk4_step(state, dt)

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Plot r(t)
# ------------------------------------------------------------

plt.figure(figsize=(10, 5))

plt.plot(t, r)

plt.xlabel("Time (s)")
plt.ylabel(r"$r(t)$ (m)")
plt.title("Spring Pendulum: Radial Motion")
plt.grid(True)

plt.show()

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Plot theta(t)
# ------------------------------------------------------------

plt.figure(figsize=(10, 5))

plt.plot(t, np.degrees(theta))

plt.xlabel("Time (s)")
plt.ylabel(r"$\theta(t)$ (degrees)")
plt.title("Spring Pendulum: Angular Motion")
plt.grid(True)

plt.show()

Curiosity

Although the equations of the spring pendulum are deterministic, the resulting trajectories can appear surprisingly intricate. Many students are astonished that such complicated patterns arise from nothing more than a mass, a spring, and gravity.

Indeed, the spring pendulum is often used as a laboratory model for nonlinear dynamics and chaos.

In the next section, we will investigate one of the most remarkable properties of the spring pendulum: the exchange of energy between radial and angular motion.

B3.6.5 Energy Exchange Between Radial and Angular Motion#

The spring pendulum has two types of motion:

  • radial motion, associated with stretching and compressing the spring,

  • angular motion, associated with swinging back and forth.

Because these motions are coupled, energy can flow between them. A motion that begins primarily as spring oscillation may gradually excite pendulum motion, and a motion that begins primarily as pendulum motion may excite radial oscillation.

This exchange of energy is one of the most important signatures of nonlinear coupling.

Box Activity — Tracking Energy Exchange

\[ T= \frac12m\dot r^2 + \frac12mr^2\dot\theta^2. \]

The potential energy is

\[ U= \frac12k(r-\ell_0)^2 - mgr\cos\theta. \]

The total energy is

\[ E=T+U. \]

  1. Compute the angular kinetic energy

\[ T_\theta=\frac12mr^2\dot\theta^2. \]

  1. Compute the spring potential energy

\[ U_s=\frac12k(r-\ell_0)^2. \]

  1. Compute the gravitational potential energy

\[ U_g=-mgr\cos\theta. \]

  1. Plot

\[ T_r, \qquad T_\theta, \qquad U_s, \qquad U_g, \qquad E \]

as functions of time.

  1. Explain how the energy moves between radial and angular motion.

Activity solution

The total energy

\[ E=T+U \]

should remain approximately constant if the numerical method and time step are appropriate.

However, the individual energy terms generally change with time. The radial kinetic energy, angular kinetic energy, spring potential energy, and gravitational potential energy exchange energy with one another.

This behavior shows that the spring and pendulum motions are not independent. The nonlinear coupling allows energy to flow between the radial and angular degrees of freedom.

A good numerical simulation should show significant variation in the individual energy components while keeping the total energy nearly constant.

In the next section, we will examine a particularly strong form of energy exchange known as internal resonance or autoparametric resonance.

B3.6.6 Internal Resonance and Autoparametric Resonance#

The spring pendulum becomes especially interesting when the radial and angular motions influence one another strongly.

For small oscillations, the system has two approximate frequencies:

\[ \omega_r \approx \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \]

for radial spring motion, and

\[ \omega_\theta \approx \sqrt{\frac{g}{r_{\mathrm{eq}}}}, \]

for angular pendulum motion, where

\[ r_{\mathrm{eq}} = \ell_0+\frac{mg}{k}. \]

A particularly strong energy exchange occurs when these frequencies satisfy approximately

\[ \omega_r \approx 2\omega_\theta. \]

This condition is known as an internal resonance because the resonance occurs between two modes of the same system.

Autoparametric Resonance

Autoparametric resonance occurs when one mode of a system parametrically excites another mode of the same system.

For the spring pendulum, strong energy exchange occurs when the radial oscillation frequency is approximately twice the angular oscillation frequency:

\[ \omega_r \approx 2\omega_\theta. \]

Box Activity — Finding the Internal Resonance Condition

Using

\[ m=0.50~\mathrm{kg}, \qquad \ell_0=0.30~\mathrm{m}, \qquad g=9.81~\mathrm{m/s^2}, \]

find the spring constant

\[ k \]

that approximately satisfies

\[ \omega_r = 2\omega_\theta. \]

Use

\[ r_{\mathrm{eq}} = \ell_0+\frac{mg}{k}, \]
\[ \omega_r = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}, \]

and

\[ \omega_\theta = \sqrt{\frac{g}{r_{\mathrm{eq}}}}. \]
Activity solution

The resonance condition is

\[ \omega_r=2\omega_\theta. \]

Substituting the approximate frequencies gives

\[ \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = 2\sqrt{\frac{g}{r_{\mathrm{eq}}}}. \]

Squaring both sides,

\[ \frac{k}{m} = \frac{4g}{r_{\mathrm{eq}}}. \]

Using

\[ r_{\mathrm{eq}} = \ell_0+\frac{mg}{k}, \]

we obtain

\[ \frac{k}{m} = \frac{4g}{\ell_0+\frac{mg}{k}}. \]

Multiplying both sides by

\[ \ell_0+\frac{mg}{k} \]

gives

\[ \frac{k\ell_0}{m} + g = 4g. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{k\ell_0}{m} = 3g, \]

so

\[ \boxed{ k = \frac{3mg}{\ell_0} } \]

Using the given values,

\[ k = \frac{3(0.50)(9.81)}{0.30} = 49.05~\mathrm{N/m}. \]

Thus, a spring constant near

\[ k\approx49~\mathrm{N/m} \]

should place the system close to the internal resonance condition.

In the next activity, we will use numerical simulations to compare motion away from resonance with motion near the autoparametric resonance condition.

B3.6.7 Numerical Exploration of Autoparametric Resonance#

The previous section showed that strong internal resonance is expected when

\[ \omega_r \approx 2\omega_\theta. \]

For the parameters

\[ m=0.50~\mathrm{kg}, \qquad \ell_0=0.30~\mathrm{m}, \]

this occurs near

\[ k\approx49~\mathrm{N/m}. \]

We now test this prediction numerically by comparing two spring pendulums:

  • one away from resonance,

  • one near the internal resonance condition.

Away from resonance, the radial and angular motions are still coupled, but the energy exchange is relatively weak. The spring length may oscillate, but the angular motion remains comparatively regular.

Near

\[ (x,y) \]

trajectory becomes more complicated.

This behavior is called autoparametric resonance because one mode of the same system acts like a parametric excitation for the other mode.

This comparison illustrates why the spring pendulum is such a powerful capstone system: the resonance condition can be estimated analytically and then tested directly through numerical simulation.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Spring pendulum: away from and near autoparametric resonance
#
# State vector:
# y = [r, r_dot, theta, theta_dot]
# ------------------------------------------------------------

# Physical parameters
m = 0.50
L0 = 0.30
g = 9.81

# Time settings
dt = 0.001
t_max = 40
t = np.arange(0, t_max, dt)

# Initial angular displacement
theta0 = np.radians(20.0)
theta_dot0 = 0.0
r_dot0 = 0.0

def derivatives(state, k):
    r, r_dot, theta, theta_dot = state

    r_ddot = (
        r*theta_dot**2
        - (k/m)*(r - L0)
        + g*np.cos(theta)
    )

    theta_ddot = (
        -2*r_dot*theta_dot/r
        - (g/r)*np.sin(theta)
    )

    return np.array([r_dot, r_ddot, theta_dot, theta_ddot])

def rk4_step(state, dt, k):
    k1 = derivatives(state, k)
    k2 = derivatives(state + 0.5*dt*k1, k)
    k3 = derivatives(state + 0.5*dt*k2, k)
    k4 = derivatives(state + dt*k3, k)

    return state + (dt/6)*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

def simulate_spring_pendulum(k):
    r_eq = L0 + m*g/k

    state = np.array([
        r_eq,
        r_dot0,
        theta0,
        theta_dot0
    ])

    r = np.zeros(len(t))
    r_dot = np.zeros(len(t))

B3.6.8 Phase Portraits of the Spring Pendulum#

The motion of the spring pendulum can be viewed from several perspectives.

Plots of

\[ r(t), \qquad \theta(t), \]

and the trajectory in the

\[ (x,y) \]

plane describe how the mass moves in physical space. Another useful viewpoint is provided by phase space.

For the spring pendulum, each degree of freedom possesses its own phase portrait:

  • radial phase space

\[ (r,\dot r), \]

  • angular phase space

\[ (\theta,\dot\theta). \]

These phase portraits reveal how the energy and geometry of the motion evolve and can provide clues about the onset of more complicated behavior.

\[ (x,y) \]

trajectories from the previous activity.

Activity solution

For

\[ k=20~\mathrm{N/m}, \]

the phase portraits are relatively regular and resemble distorted closed curves. Although the radial and angular motions are coupled, the energy exchange remains modest.

Near the internal resonance condition,

\[ k=49~\mathrm{N/m}, \]

the coupling between the two modes becomes much stronger. The radial phase portrait generally exhibits larger excursions and a more complicated structure because energy is transferred more efficiently between the radial and angular motions.

The angular phase portrait also changes, although the effect is often less dramatic than in the radial motion.

Comparing these phase portraits with the corresponding

\[ (x,y) \]

trajectories illustrates an important lesson: two motions that may look similar in physical space can have very different structures in phase space.

Thus, phase portraits provide information about the dynamics that is not always apparent from ordinary position plots.

B3.6.9 Sensitivity to Initial Conditions#

The previous activities showed that the spring pendulum can produce rich trajectories, strong energy exchange, and complicated phase portraits. We now ask whether the system can also exhibit one of the defining signatures of chaos: sensitivity to initial conditions.

Rather than changing the physical parameters, we will keep the same system and make only a tiny change to the initial angle.

Box Activity — Sensitive Dependence in the Spring Pendulum

Use the spring pendulum with

\[ m=0.50~\mathrm{kg}, \qquad \ell_0=0.30~\mathrm{m}, \qquad g=9.81~\mathrm{m/s^2}, \]

and choose the near-resonance value

\[ k=49~\mathrm{N/m}. \]

Compare two simulations.

Case A#

\[ r(0)=r_{\mathrm{eq}}, \qquad \dot r(0)=0, \]
\[ \theta(0)=20.000^\circ, \qquad \dot\theta(0)=0. \]

Case B#

\[ r(0)=r_{\mathrm{eq}}, \qquad \dot r(0)=0, \]
\[ \theta(0)=20.001^\circ, \qquad \dot\theta(0)=0. \]

Your script should:

  1. Solve the equations of motion for both initial conditions.

  2. Plot both versions of

\[ \theta(t) \]

on the same graph.

  1. Plot the difference

\[ |\Delta\theta(t)| = |\theta_B(t)-\theta_A(t)|. \]

  1. Plot both trajectories in the

\[ (x,y) \]

plane.

  1. Determine whether the two motions remain close together or eventually separate.

Activity solution

At early times, the two simulations should be nearly indistinguishable because their initial angles differ by only

\[ 0.001^\circ. \]

If the motion remains regular, the difference

\[ |\Delta\theta(t)| \]

will remain small or grow only modestly.

If the motion is chaotic, the small initial difference may grow substantially with time. The two trajectories may eventually appear unrelated, even though the equations and parameters are identical.

This does not mean the system is random. The spring pendulum is still governed by deterministic equations. The apparent unpredictability arises because tiny differences in initial conditions can be amplified by the nonlinear dynamics.

For the parameter values used here, the two trajectories may remain close for the duration of the simulation. In that case, the conclusion is not that the spring pendulum is chaotic, but rather that this particular motion is regular or only weakly sensitive to the chosen perturbation.

This is an important lesson: nonlinear does not automatically mean chaotic. Chaos depends on the energy, parameters, and initial conditions.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Spring pendulum: sensitive dependence on initial conditions
#
# Compare two simulations that differ only by 0.001 degrees
# in the initial angle.
# ------------------------------------------------------------

# Physical parameters
m = 0.50
L0 = 0.30
g = 9.81
k = 49.0

# Equilibrium length
r_eq = L0 + m*g/k

# Time settings
dt = 0.001
t_max = 60
t = np.arange(0, t_max, dt)

def derivatives(state):
    r, r_dot, theta, theta_dot = state

    r_ddot = (
        r*theta_dot**2
        - (k/m)*(r - L0)
        + g*np.cos(theta)
    )

    theta_ddot = (
        -2*r_dot*theta_dot/r
        - (g/r)*np.sin(theta)
    )

    return np.array([r_dot, r_ddot, theta_dot, theta_ddot])

def rk4_step(state, dt):
    k1 = derivatives(state)
    k2 = derivatives(state + 0.5*dt*k1)
    k3 = derivatives(state + 0.5*dt*k2)
    k4 = derivatives(state + dt*k3)

    return state + (dt/6)*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

def simulate(theta0_deg):
    state = np.array([
        r_eq,
        0.0,
        np.radians(theta0_deg),
        0.0
    ])

    r = np.zeros(len(t))
    r_dot = np.zeros(len(t))
    theta = np.zeros(len(t))
    theta_dot = np.zeros(len(t))

    for i in range(len(t)):
        r[i] = state[0]
        r_dot[i] = state[1]
        theta[i] = state[2]
        theta_dot[i] = state[3]

        state = rk4_step(state, dt)

    x = r*np.sin(theta)
    y = -r*np.cos(theta)

    return r, r_dot, theta, theta_dot, x, y

B3.6.10 Poincaré Sections of the Spring Pendulum#

As the motion of the spring pendulum becomes increasingly complicated, ordinary trajectories and phase portraits may become difficult to interpret.

A powerful way to simplify the dynamics is to construct a Poincaré section. Rather than displaying the entire trajectory, we record the state of the system whenever a specified condition is satisfied.

For the spring pendulum, one convenient choice is to record the radial state

\[ (r,\dot r) \]

whenever

\[ \theta=0 \]

and

\[ \dot\theta>0. \]

Thus, each point corresponds to a crossing of the downward vertical with the mass moving in the same direction.

Poincaré Section of the Spring Pendulum

A Poincaré section for the spring pendulum may be constructed by recording

\[ (r,\dot r) \]

whenever

\[ \theta=0 \]

and

\[ \dot\theta>0. \]

The resulting pattern provides a compact description of the long-term dynamics.

Because the spring pendulum is not externally driven, there is no natural driving period. Instead, the section is defined geometrically by choosing a surface in phase space.

  1. Compare the resulting pattern with the ordinary radial phase portrait.

  2. Determine whether the section consists primarily of isolated points, closed curves, or a scattered cloud of points.

Activity solution

For the parameter values used here, the Poincaré section typically consists of a relatively simple collection of points or smooth curves rather than a complicated cloud.

This indicates that the motion remains largely regular, even though the system is highly nonlinear and exhibits strong energy exchange between radial and angular motion.

A truly chaotic motion would produce a much more complicated structure containing a broad cloud of points rather than smooth curves.

This illustrates an important lesson of nonlinear dynamics:

Nonlinear Does Not Necessarily Mean Chaotic

Nonlinearity can produce complicated trajectories, internal resonance, and strong coupling without leading to chaos.

Chaos depends not only on the form of the equations but also on the parameters, energy, and initial conditions.

Thus, the Poincaré section provides a powerful tool for distinguishing regular motion from chaotic motion.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Poincaré section of the spring pendulum
#
# Record (r, r_dot) whenever:
#
#     theta crosses 0 upward
#     theta_dot > 0
# ------------------------------------------------------------

# Physical parameters
m = 0.50
L0 = 0.30
g = 9.81
k = 49.0

# Equilibrium length
r_eq = L0 + m*g/k

# Time settings
dt = 0.001
t_max = 200
t = np.arange(0, t_max, dt)

# Initial conditions
r0 = r_eq
r_dot0 = 0.0
theta0 = np.radians(20.0)
theta_dot0 = 0.0

def derivatives(state):
    r, r_dot, theta, theta_dot = state

    r_ddot = (
        r*theta_dot**2
        - (k/m)*(r - L0)
        + g*np.cos(theta)
    )

    theta_ddot = (
        -2*r_dot*theta_dot/r
        - (g/r)*np.sin(theta)
    )

    return np.array([r_dot, r_ddot, theta_dot, theta_ddot])

def rk4_step(state, dt):
    k1 = derivatives(state)
    k2 = derivatives(state + 0.5*dt*k1)
    k3 = derivatives(state + 0.5*dt*k2)
    k4 = derivatives(state + dt*k3)

    return state + (dt/6)*(k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Numerical integration
# ------------------------------------------------------------

r = np.zeros(len(t))
r_dot = np.zeros(len(t))
theta = np.zeros(len(t))
theta_dot = np.zeros(len(t))

state = np.array([r0, r_dot0, theta0, theta_dot0])

for i in range(len(t)):
    r[i] = state[0]
    r_dot[i] = state[1]
    theta[i] = state[2]
    theta_dot[i] = state[3]

    state = rk4_step(state, dt)

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Construct Poincaré section
# ------------------------------------------------------------

r_section = []
r_dot_section = []

for i in range(len(t)-1):

    # Upward crossing of theta = 0
    if theta[i] < 0 and theta[i+1] >= 0 and theta_dot[i] > 0:

        # Linear interpolation for a cleaner crossing estimate
        frac = -theta[i] / (theta[i+1] - theta[i])

        r_cross = r[i] + frac*(r[i+1] - r[i])
        r_dot_cross = r_dot[i] + frac*(r_dot[i+1] - r_dot[i])

        r_section.append(r_cross)
        r_dot_section.append(r_dot_cross)

r_section = np.array(r_section)
r_dot_section = np.array(r_dot_section)

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Plot radial phase portrait
# ------------------------------------------------------------

plt.figure(figsize=(8, 6))

plt.plot(r, r_dot, linewidth=0.7)

plt.xlabel(r"$r$ (m)")
plt.ylabel(r"$\dot r$ (m/s)")
plt.title("Spring Pendulum: Radial Phase Portrait")
plt.grid(True)

plt.show()

# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Plot Poincaré section
# ------------------------------------------------------------

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 7))

plt.plot(r_section, r_dot_section, ".", markersize=4)

plt.xlabel(r"$r$ (m)")
plt.ylabel(r"$\dot r$ (m/s)")
plt.title(r"Poincaré Section: $(r,\dot r)$ at $\theta=0$, $\dot\theta>0$")
plt.grid(True)

plt.show()

B3.6.11 Summary and Reflection#

The spring pendulum provides a remarkable example of how rich behavior can arise from a deceptively simple mechanical system. Beginning with a mass attached to a spring, we developed a mathematical model using Lagrangian mechanics and used numerical methods to explore its motion.

Along the way, we encountered many of the themes that have appeared throughout this module:

  • generalized coordinates,

  • Lagrange’s equations,

  • coupled oscillators,

  • numerical integration,

  • trajectories in physical space,

  • phase portraits,

  • energy exchange,

  • internal resonance,

  • and Poincaré sections.

The Spring Pendulum as a Microcosm of Nonlinear Dynamics

The spring pendulum illustrates many of the key ideas of nonlinear dynamics within a single physical system.

These include

  • coupled motion,
  • nonlinear interactions,
  • energy transfer between modes,
  • autoparametric resonance,
  • phase-space methods,
  • and the possibility of chaotic behavior.

In this sense, the spring pendulum serves as a miniature laboratory for modern dynamical systems.

One of the most important lessons of this project is that nonlinear systems need not be chaotic. Throughout our investigation, we found complicated trajectories, strong energy exchange, and intricate phase portraits. Yet these features alone do not imply chaos.

Instead, chaos is only one possible outcome of nonlinear dynamics. Many nonlinear systems exhibit rich and beautiful behavior while remaining largely regular.

Important Lesson

Complicated behavior does not necessarily imply chaos.

Nonlinear systems may display resonance, energy exchange, and intricate trajectories while remaining regular and predictable.

Chaos depends not only on the equations themselves, but also on the parameters, energy, and initial conditions.

Throughout this chapter, we approached the spring pendulum not as a single end-of-chapter problem but as a scientific investigation. We derived the equations, developed numerical models, visualized the motion, and interpreted the results. In doing so, we followed many of the same steps used by physicists and engineers when studying real systems.

Looking Beyond This Module

The ideas encountered throughout this chapter represent only a small glimpse into the broader world of nonlinear dynamics.

Modern research continues to explore

  • chaos and strange attractors,
  • pattern formation,
  • network synchronization,
  • biological oscillations,
  • nonlinear waves and solitons,
  • turbulence,
  • complex systems,
  • and the dynamics of coupled mechanical systems.

Many of these topics remain active areas of research and continue to reveal unexpected connections between mathematics, physics, engineering, and the natural world.

The study of classical mechanics often begins with idealized systems and simple equations. Yet, as we have seen, even Newton’s laws can lead to an astonishing richness of behavior. From simple harmonic motion to synchronization and chaos, classical mechanics provides a gateway to some of the most beautiful and profound ideas in all of physics.
