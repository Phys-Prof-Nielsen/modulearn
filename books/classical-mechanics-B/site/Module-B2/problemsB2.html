Problem 1 - Normal Modes of a Finite Oscillator Chain#

Consider a chain of \(N\) identical masses connected by identical springs. Each mass has mass \(m\), each spring has spring constant \(k\), and the equilibrium spacing between neighboring masses is \(a\).

Assume the ends of the chain are fixed, so that

\[ u_0(t)=0, \qquad u_{N+1}(t)=0. \]

The equation of motion for the interior masses is

\[ m\ddot u_n = k(u_{n+1}-2u_n+u_{n-1}), \qquad n=1,2,\dots,N. \]

Assume a normal-mode solution of the form

\[ u_n(t)=A\sin(qna)\cos(\omega t). \]

  1. Substitute this trial solution into the discrete equation of motion.

  2. Show that the allowed frequencies satisfy

    \[ \omega^2 = \frac{4k}{m} \sin^2\left(\frac{qa}{2}\right). \]

  3. Use the fixed-end boundary conditions to show that the allowed wave numbers are

    \[ q_j = \frac{j\pi}{(N+1)a}, \qquad j=1,2,\dots,N. \]

  4. Write the normal-mode frequencies \(\omega_j\).

  5. Explain what happens to the allowed frequencies as \(N\) becomes very large.

Problem solution

Part 1

Using

\[ u_n(t)=A\sin(qna)\cos(\omega t), \]

we have

\[ \ddot u_n(t) = -\omega^2A\sin(qna)\cos(\omega t). \]

Also,

\[ u_{n+1} = A\sin(q(n+1)a)\cos(\omega t), \]

and

\[ u_{n-1} = A\sin(q(n-1)a)\cos(\omega t). \]

Part 2

Substituting into the equation of motion gives

\[ -m\omega^2A\sin(qna)\cos(\omega t) = kA\cos(\omega t) [ \sin(q(n+1)a)-2\sin(qna)+\sin(q(n-1)a) ]. \]

Using the identity

\[ \sin(\alpha+\beta)+\sin(\alpha-\beta) = 2\sin\alpha\cos\beta, \]

we find

\[ \sin(q(n+1)a)+\sin(q(n-1)a) = 2\sin(qna)\cos(qa). \]

Therefore,

\[ \sin(q(n+1)a)-2\sin(qna)+\sin(q(n-1)a) = 2\sin(qna)\cos(qa)-2\sin(qna). \]

So

\[ -m\omega^2\sin(qna) = 2k\sin(qna)[\cos(qa)-1]. \]

Canceling the common factor gives

\[ m\omega^2 = 2k[1-\cos(qa)]. \]

Using

\[ 1-\cos(qa) = 2\sin^2\left(\frac{qa}{2}\right), \]

we obtain

\[ \omega^2 = \frac{4k}{m} \sin^2\left(\frac{qa}{2}\right). \]

Part 3

The left boundary condition is automatically satisfied because

\[ u_0(t)=A\sin(0)\cos(\omega t)=0. \]

The right boundary condition requires

\[ u_{N+1}(t)=A\sin(q(N+1)a)\cos(\omega t)=0. \]

For a nonzero mode,

\[ \sin(q(N+1)a)=0. \]

Thus,

\[ q(N+1)a=j\pi, \]

so

\[ q_j = \frac{j\pi}{(N+1)a}, \qquad j=1,2,\dots,N. \]

Part 4

The normal-mode frequencies are therefore

\[ \boxed{ \omega_j = 2\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} \sin\left(\frac{j\pi}{2(N+1)}\right) } \]

for

\[ j=1,2,\dots,N. \]

Part 5

As \(N\) becomes very large, the allowed values of \(q_j\) become more closely spaced. The discrete spectrum of normal-mode frequencies begins to resemble a continuous band of possible wave frequencies. This is one way the finite oscillator chain begins to approach the behavior of a continuous wave medium.

Problem 2 - Frequency Ratio Identification#

A Lissajous figure shows three horizontal lobes and two vertical lobes.

The curve is stable and closed.

  1. What is the frequency ratio \(\omega_x : \omega_y\)?

  2. If the phase difference increases, what geometric change will occur to the curve?

Problem solution

Part 1 — Determine the Frequency Ratio

For a closed Lissajous figure, the number of horizontal and vertical lobes reveals the ratio of the frequencies.

Since the figure has

  • 3 horizontal lobes,

  • 2 vertical lobes,

the frequency ratio is

\[ \boxed{ \omega_x : \omega_y = 3 : 2 } \]

Part 2 — Effect of Increasing the Phase Difference

Changing the phase difference

\[ \Delta\phi=\phi_y-\phi_x \]

does not alter the frequency ratio.

Therefore,

  • the number of lobes remains unchanged,

  • the curve remains closed,

  • the overall geometry is rotated, skewed, or twisted.

As \(\Delta\phi\) changes, overlapping portions of the curve may separate and the figure may appear to unfold or rotate within the plane.

Thus, increasing the phase difference changes the orientation and shape of the figure but not its fundamental lobe structure (topological structure).

\[ \boxed{ \text{Changing }\Delta\phi\text{ alters the geometry of the curve but does not change the number of lobes.} } \]

Problem 3 - Phase Difference From a Lissajous Shape#

Two signals produce a Lissajous figure described as leaning to the right, with no symmetry about the axes.

The frequency ratio is

\[ 1:1. \]

  1. What does the right-leaning pattern imply about \(\Delta\phi\)?

  2. If instead the figure were a perfect vertical line, what would \(\Delta\phi\) be?

Problem solution

Part 1 — Interpret the Right-Leaning Pattern

For equal frequencies,

\[ x(t)=A\cos(\omega t), \qquad y(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\Delta\phi). \]

A right-tilted ellipse indicates that the \(y\)-signal reaches a given phase before the \(x\)-signal.

Therefore, the \(y\)-signal leads the \(x\)-signal, implying

\[ \boxed{ \Delta\phi>0. } \]

Thus, the phase difference is positive.

Part 2 — Determine the Phase Difference for a Vertical Line

A perfect vertical line occurs when the two oscillations are exactly in phase.

In this case,

\[ y(t)=x(t), \]

so

\[ \boxed{ \Delta\phi=0. } \]

Therefore, the two signals oscillate together with no phase difference.

Problem 4 - Coupled Oscillator Interpretation#

A two-mass mechanical system has normal-mode frequencies

\[ \omega_1=2.0~\text{rad/s} \]

and

\[ \omega_2=3.0~\text{rad/s}. \]

The system is displaced so that both modes are present in each coordinate.

  1. Will the Lissajous figure in \((x,y)\) space be closed or non-closed?

  2. How many cycles of each mode occur before the pattern repeats?

Problem solution

Part 1 — Determine Whether the Pattern is Closed

A Lissajous-type pattern closes only if the frequency ratio is rational. Here,

\[ \frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = \frac{2.0}{3.0} = \frac{2}{3}. \]

Since

\[ \frac{2}{3} \]

is the ratio of two integers, the frequency ratio is rational.

Therefore, after enough time, both oscillations return to their starting phases at the same time. The figure is closed:

\[ \boxed{ \text{The Lissajous figure is closed.} } \]

Part 2 — Determine the Number of Cycles Before Repeating

The pattern repeats when each mode has completed an integer number of cycles.

The period of mode 1 is

\[ T_1=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_1} = \frac{2\pi}{2.0} = \pi. \]

The period of mode 2 is

\[ T_2=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_2} = \frac{2\pi}{3.0}. \]

We need the smallest time \(T\) such that

\[ T=N_1T_1=N_2T_2, \]

where \(N_1\) and \(N_2\) are integers.

Using the frequencies directly, the number of cycles completed by each mode during time \(T\) is

\[ N_1=\frac{\omega_1T}{2\pi}, \qquad N_2=\frac{\omega_2T}{2\pi}. \]

The smallest repeat time is

\[ T=2\pi. \]

Then mode 1 completes

\[ N_1 = \frac{\omega_1T}{2\pi} = \frac{(2.0)(2\pi)}{2\pi} = 2 \]

cycles, while mode 2 completes

\[ N_2 = \frac{\omega_2T}{2\pi} = \frac{(3.0)(2\pi)}{2\pi} = 3 \]

cycles.

Thus, the pattern repeats after

\[ \boxed{ \text{2 cycles of mode 1 and 3 cycles of mode 2.} } \]

The faster mode completes more cycles before the figure repeats.

Problem 5 - Rational vs. Irrational Ratio: Plotting#

Consider

\[ x(t)=\cos(3t), \qquad y(t)=\cos\left(4t+\frac{\pi}{4}\right), \]

and

\[ x(t)=\cos(t), \qquad y(t)=\cos\left(\sqrt{2}\,t+\frac{\pi}{4}\right). \]

  1. Which motion produces a closed curve?

  2. Which motion produces a quasi-periodic curve?

  3. Produce a Python script showing both plots side-by-side.

Problem solution

Part 1 — Identify the Closed Curve

For a Lissajous figure to close, the frequency ratio must be rational.

For the first motion,

\[ x(t)=\cos(3t), \qquad y(t)=\cos\left(4t+\frac{\pi}{4}\right), \]

the frequencies are

\[ \omega_x=3, \qquad \omega_y=4. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{\omega_x}{\omega_y} = \frac{3}{4}. \]

Since \(3/4\) is rational, the motion eventually repeats exactly.

Thus, the first motion produces a closed curve:

\[ \boxed{ x(t)=\cos(3t), \qquad y(t)=\cos\left(4t+\frac{\pi}{4}\right) \text{ is closed.} } \]

Part 2 — Identify the Quasi-Periodic Curve

For the second motion,

\[ x(t)=\cos(t), \qquad y(t)=\cos\left(\sqrt{2}\,t+\frac{\pi}{4}\right), \]

the frequencies are

\[ \omega_x=1, \qquad \omega_y=\sqrt{2}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{\omega_x}{\omega_y} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}. \]

Because \(\sqrt{2}\) is irrational, this ratio cannot be written as a ratio of two integers.

Therefore, the motion never returns exactly to the same phase relationship. The curve does not close, and instead traces a quasi-periodic pattern:

\[ \boxed{ x(t)=\cos(t), \qquad y(t)=\cos\left(\sqrt{2}\,t+\frac{\pi}{4}\right) \text{ is quasi-periodic.} } \]

Part 3 — Python Plotting Script

The Python script should generate both trajectories over a sufficiently long time interval and display them side-by-side.

The first plot should show a closed Lissajous figure because the frequency ratio is rational:

\[ 3:4. \]

The second plot should show a curve that continues to fill the available region without closing exactly because the frequency ratio is irrational:

\[ 1:\sqrt{2}. \]

When plotting the second case, increasing the maximum time will cause the curve to fill in more densely, but it will not become exactly periodic.

%reset -f
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

t1 = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 3000)
t2 = np.linspace(0, 40*np.pi, 8000)

# closed case: 3:4

y1 = np.cos(4*t1 + np.pi/4)

y2 = np.cos(np.sqrt(2)*t2 + np.pi/4)

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10,5))

Problem 6 - Determining Phase From Oscilloscope Data#

A Lissajous figure on an oscilloscope shows an ellipse, tilted upward to the right.

Both signals have the same frequency.

  1. Is \(\Delta\phi\) positive or negative?

  2. If the ellipse collapsed into a straight line with positive slope, what would \(\Delta\phi\) be?

  3. Create a Python script plotting an equal-frequency ellipse with \(\Delta\phi=\pi/3\).

Problem solution

Part 1 — Determine the Sign of the Phase Difference

For equal-frequency signals, we may write

\[ x(t)=A\cos(\omega t), \]

and

\[ y(t)=B\cos(\omega t+\Delta\phi). \]

A tilted ellipse indicates that the two signals are neither exactly in phase nor exactly out of phase.

If the ellipse is tilted upward to the right, then the \(y\)-signal reaches its corresponding phase slightly before the \(x\)-signal. In this convention, \(y(t)\) leads \(x(t)\).

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \Delta\phi>0. } \]

Part 2 — Straight Line With Positive Slope

If the ellipse collapses into a straight line, then the two signals have no phase offset except possibly \(0\) or \(\pi\).

A straight line with positive slope means that \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\) increase and decrease together.

Thus, the two signals are in phase:

\[ y(t)\propto x(t). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \Delta\phi=0. } \]

A straight line with negative slope would instead correspond to signals that are out of phase by \(\pi\).

Part 3 — Python Plotting Script

The Python script should plot

\[ x(t)=\cos(\omega t), \]

and

\[ y(t)=\cos\left(\omega t+\frac{\pi}{3}\right). \]

Since the two frequencies are equal, the curve will be an ellipse rather than a multi-lobed Lissajous figure. The nonzero phase difference

\[ \Delta\phi=\frac{\pi}{3} \]

causes the ellipse to open up rather than collapse into a straight line.

import numpy as np

Problem 7 - Two Given Input Signals and Lissajous Geometry#

Two signals are fed into the horizontal and vertical inputs of an oscilloscope:

\[ x(t)=2\cos\left(5t-\frac{\pi}{6}\right), \]

and

\[ y(t)=3\cos\left(3t+\frac{\pi}{3}\right). \]

  1. Determine the frequency ratio \(\omega_x:\omega_y\).

  2. Compute the phase difference \(\Delta\phi=\phi_y-\phi_x\).

  3. Based on the frequency ratio, will the Lissajous figure be closed or quasi-periodic?

  4. Without plotting, predict the number of horizontal and vertical lobes.

  5. Write a Python script to plot the resulting Lissajous figure.

Problem solution

Part 1 — Determine the Frequency Ratio

The angular frequency is the coefficient multiplying \(t\) inside the cosine function.

For the horizontal signal,

\[ x(t)=2\cos\left(5t-\frac{\pi}{6}\right), \]

so

\[ \omega_x=5. \]

For the vertical signal,

\[ y(t)=3\cos\left(3t+\frac{\pi}{3}\right), \]

so

\[ \omega_y=3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \omega_x:\omega_y=5:3. } \]

Part 2 — Compute the Phase Difference

The phase constants are the terms added to the arguments of the cosine functions.

For the horizontal signal,

\[ \phi_x=-\frac{\pi}{6}. \]

For the vertical signal,

\[ \phi_y=\frac{\pi}{3}. \]

The phase difference is

\[ \Delta\phi=\phi_y-\phi_x. \]

Substituting,

\[ \Delta\phi = \frac{\pi}{3} - \left(-\frac{\pi}{6}\right). \]

Therefore,

\[ \Delta\phi = \frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{2\pi}{6} + \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{3\pi}{6} = \frac{\pi}{2}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \Delta\phi=\frac{\pi}{2}. } \]

Since \(\Delta\phi>0\), the vertical signal leads the horizontal signal by \(90^\circ\).

Part 3 — Determine Whether the Curve is Closed or Quasi-Periodic

A Lissajous figure closes when the frequency ratio is rational.

Here,

\[ \frac{\omega_x}{\omega_y} = \frac{5}{3}. \]

Since \(5/3\) is the ratio of two integers, the ratio is rational.

Therefore, the pattern eventually repeats exactly, and the curve is closed:

\[ \boxed{ \text{The Lissajous figure is closed.} } \]

Part 4 — Predict the Number of Lobes

The ratio

\[ \omega_x:\omega_y=5:3 \]

means that the horizontal oscillation completes 5 cycles while the vertical oscillation completes 3 cycles before the full pattern repeats.

Using the lobe-count convention from this module:

  • the number associated with \(\omega_x\) gives the horizontal lobe count,

  • the number associated with \(\omega_y\) gives the vertical lobe count.

Therefore, the figure has

\[ \boxed{ 5\ \text{horizontal lobes} } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ 3\ \text{vertical lobes}. } \]

The different amplitudes,

\[ A_x=2, \qquad A_y=3, \]

stretch the figure vertically relative to horizontally, but they do not change the number of lobes.

The phase difference

\[ \Delta\phi=\frac{\pi}{2} \]

changes the orientation and geometry of the pattern, but it does not change the frequency ratio.

Part 5 — Python Plotting Script

The Python script should plot the parametric curve

\[ x(t)=2\cos\left(5t-\frac{\pi}{6}\right), \]

and

\[ y(t)=3\cos\left(3t+\frac{\pi}{3}\right). \]

Since the frequency ratio is rational, the plot should close after a finite time. A convenient choice is to plot over one common repeat interval.

Because the frequencies are \(5\) and \(3\), the motion repeats when both phases have completed integer numbers of cycles. The smallest repeat time is

\[ T=2\pi. \]

Therefore, plotting over

\[ 0\le t\le 2\pi \]

is sufficient to show one complete Lissajous figure.

The resulting plot should show a closed \(5:3\) Lissajous figure, stretched vertically because \(A_y>A_x\), and shaped by the phase difference \(\Delta\phi=\pi/2\).

Problem 8 - Biomechanics: Coordinated Joint Motion#

During a gait-analysis study, reflective markers are placed on the hip and knee of a subject’s right leg.

The joint angles, measured in degrees during steady walking, are modeled by

\[ \theta_{\text{hip}}(t)=20+8\cos\left(2t-\frac{\pi}{12}\right), \]

and

\[ \theta_{\text{knee}}(t)=40+15\cos\left(3t+\frac{\pi}{4}\right), \]

where \(t\) is measured in seconds.

  1. Remove the constant offsets and write the oscillatory parts of both signals.

  2. Determine the frequency ratio between hip and knee oscillations.

  3. Will the resulting Lissajous curve, hip angle vs. knee angle, be closed or quasi-periodic?

  4. Predict qualitatively what the curve will look like, including the number of lobes, symmetry, and drift.

  5. Write a Python script that plots the Lissajous figure for hip vs. knee angle over an interval of your choosing.

Problem solution

Part 1 — Remove the Constant Offsets

The constant terms represent the average joint angles about which each joint oscillates.

For the hip angle,

\[ \theta_{\text{hip}}(t)=20+8\cos\left(2t-\frac{\pi}{12}\right). \]

Removing the constant offset \(20^\circ\) gives the oscillatory part

\[ \boxed{ \theta_{\text{hip, osc}}(t)=8\cos\left(2t-\frac{\pi}{12}\right). } \]

For the knee angle,

\[ \theta_{\text{knee}}(t)=40+15\cos\left(3t+\frac{\pi}{4}\right). \]

Removing the constant offset \(40^\circ\) gives

\[ \boxed{ \theta_{\text{knee, osc}}(t)=15\cos\left(3t+\frac{\pi}{4}\right). } \]

The offsets shift the center of the curve in the hip-knee angle plane, but they do not affect whether the curve is closed or quasi-periodic.

Part 2 — Determine the Frequency Ratio

The angular frequency is the coefficient multiplying \(t\) inside the cosine.

For the hip motion,

\[ \omega_{\text{hip}}=2. \]

For the knee motion,

\[ \omega_{\text{knee}}=3. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \omega_{\text{hip}}:\omega_{\text{knee}}=2:3. } \]

This means that during the same repeat interval, the hip oscillation completes 2 cycles while the knee oscillation completes 3 cycles.

Part 3 — Determine Whether the Curve is Closed or Quasi-Periodic

A Lissajous curve is closed if the frequency ratio is rational.

Here,

\[ \frac{\omega_{\text{hip}}}{\omega_{\text{knee}}} = \frac{2}{3}. \]

Since \(2/3\) is the ratio of two integers, the motion is rationally related.

Therefore, the hip-knee curve repeats exactly after a finite time:

\[ \boxed{ \text{The Lissajous curve is closed.} } \]

Part 4 — Predict the Qualitative Shape

Using the lobe-count convention from this module, the ratio

\[ \omega_{\text{hip}}:\omega_{\text{knee}}=2:3 \]

corresponds to

\[ \boxed{ 2\ \text{horizontal lobes} } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ 3\ \text{vertical lobes}. } \]

If the hip angle is plotted on the horizontal axis and the knee angle is plotted on the vertical axis, the horizontal direction represents hip motion and the vertical direction represents knee motion.

The phase constants are

\[ \phi_{\text{hip}}=-\frac{\pi}{12}, \]

and

\[ \phi_{\text{knee}}=\frac{\pi}{4}. \]

The phase difference is

\[ \Delta\phi = \phi_{\text{knee}}-\phi_{\text{hip}}. \]

Substituting,

\[ \Delta\phi = \frac{\pi}{4} - \left(-\frac{\pi}{12}\right) = \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{\pi}{12}. \]

Using a common denominator,

\[ \Delta\phi = \frac{3\pi}{12} + \frac{\pi}{12} = \frac{4\pi}{12} = \frac{\pi}{3}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \Delta\phi=\frac{\pi}{3}. } \]

Since the knee phase is ahead of the hip phase, the knee oscillation leads the hip oscillation.

The amplitude of the knee oscillation is larger than the amplitude of the hip oscillation:

\[ 15^\circ>8^\circ. \]

Therefore, the curve will be stretched more in the vertical direction than in the horizontal direction.

The phase difference will skew or tilt the pattern, while the rational frequency ratio ensures that the curve remains closed. Since this is a steady walking model with a rational ratio, the curve should not drift from cycle to cycle.

Qualitatively, the curve should look like a closed, skewed \(2:3\) Lissajous figure centered at

\[ (\theta_{\text{hip}},\theta_{\text{knee}})=(20^\circ,40^\circ). \]

Part 5 — Python Plotting Script

The Python script should plot hip angle on the horizontal axis and knee angle on the vertical axis:

\[ x(t)=\theta_{\text{hip}}(t), \]

and

\[ y(t)=\theta_{\text{knee}}(t). \]

Since the frequencies are \(2\) and \(3\), the smallest repeat time is

\[ T=2\pi. \]

During this time, the hip completes

\[ \frac{(2)(2\pi)}{2\pi}=2 \]

cycles, while the knee completes

\[ \frac{(3)(2\pi)}{2\pi}=3 \]

cycles.

Thus, plotting over

\[ 0\le t\le 2\pi \]

shows one complete hip-knee Lissajous loop.

In a biomechanics context, a stable closed loop in hip-knee angle space indicates consistent phase-locked joint coordination during steady walking. If the subject’s gait were changing, fatigued, or irregular, the loop might drift, deform, or fail to close cleanly from cycle to cycle.

Problem solution

Part 1 — Relation to Ordinary Lissajous Figures

An ordinary Lissajous figure is produced by two perpendicular oscillations of the form

\[ x(t)=A_x\cos(\omega_x t+\phi_x), \]

and

\[ y(t)=A_y\cos(\omega_y t+\phi_y). \]

The harmonograph model is similar, except that each coordinate includes an exponential damping factor:

\[ e^{-d_x t} \]

and

\[ e^{-d_y t}. \]

If the damping constants were zero,

\[ d_x=d_y=0, \]

then

\[ e^{-d_x t}=e^{-d_y t}=1, \]

and the harmonograph equations would reduce to the standard Lissajous equations.

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \text{A harmonograph is a damped Lissajous system.} } \]

The frequency ratio and phase difference still control the geometric structure, but damping causes the amplitude to change over time.

Part 2 — Closed Curve or Inward Spiral

The given damping constants are nonzero:

\[ d_x=d_y=0.02. \]

Therefore, the amplitudes decrease with time according to

\[ A_x e^{-d_x t} \]

and

\[ A_y e^{-d_y t}. \]

Because the amplitudes are continually shrinking, the curve cannot retrace itself exactly. Even though the frequency ratio is rational,

\[ \omega_x:\omega_y=3:4, \]

the trajectory changes size during each cycle.

Thus, the drawing spirals inward toward the origin:

\[ \boxed{ \text{The curve spirals inward rather than closing exactly.} } \]

The rational frequency ratio would produce a closed Lissajous curve only if the damping were absent.

Part 3 — Predict the Number of Lobes

Ignoring damping for a moment, the frequency ratio is

\[ \omega_x:\omega_y=3:4. \]

Using the lobe-count convention from this module, this corresponds to

\[ \boxed{ 3\ \text{horizontal lobes} } \]

and

\[ \boxed{ 4\ \text{vertical lobes}. } \]

Damping does not change the underlying frequency ratio, so the main looping structure still reflects a \(3:4\) pattern.

However, because the amplitude shrinks continuously, the loops do not perfectly overlap from one cycle to the next. Instead, the curve winds inward through a sequence of smaller and smaller loops.

Part 4 — Qualitative Effect of Damping

In an undamped Lissajous figure, the same path is traced repeatedly. If the frequency ratio is rational, the curve is closed and repeats exactly.

With damping, the amplitudes decrease with time. This causes several visible changes:

  • The outer loops appear first and are largest.

  • Later loops are smaller and lie closer to the origin.

  • The curve forms a spiral-like or rosette-like drawing.

  • The path does not close exactly, even though the frequency ratio is rational.

  • The pattern gradually fades inward toward the center.

The phase difference

\[ \phi_y-\phi_x=\frac{\pi}{2} \]

also affects the orientation and symmetry of the pattern.

Thus, the harmonograph begins like a \(3:4\) Lissajous figure, but the exponential damping turns it into an inward-winding artistic pattern.

Problem 13 - Parametric Resonance: Growth Near \(\Omega=2\omega_0\)#

A parametrically driven oscillator satisfies

\[ \ddot{x} + \omega_0^2\left(1+h\cos(\Omega t)\right)x = 0, \]

where \(\omega_0\) is the natural frequency, \(h\) is the modulation strength, and \(\Omega\) is the modulation frequency.

Suppose

\[ \omega_0=2.0~\text{rad/s}, \qquad h=0.10. \]

  1. Determine the modulation frequency \(\Omega\) that produces the strongest parametric resonance.

  2. Estimate the exponential growth rate \(\lambda\) using

    \[ \lambda\approx\frac{h\omega_0}{4}. \]

  3. If the initial amplitude is

    \[ A_0=0.010~\text{m}, \]

    estimate the amplitude after

    \[ t=40~\text{s}, \]

    assuming exponential growth.

  4. Explain why exact initial conditions

    \[ x(0)=0, \qquad \dot{x}(0)=0 \]

    would not lead to growth.

  5. Describe how damping would change the behavior.

Problem solution

Part 1 — Determine the Resonance Condition

For parametric resonance, the strongest growth occurs when the modulation frequency is approximately twice the natural frequency:

\[ \Omega\approx2\omega_0. \]

Since

\[ \omega_0=2.0~\text{rad/s}, \]

we find

\[ \Omega = 2(2.0) = 4.0~\text{rad/s}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \Omega\approx4.0~\text{rad/s}. } \]

Part 2 — Estimate the Growth Rate

For weak modulation near exact parametric resonance, the approximate growth rate is

\[ \lambda\approx\frac{h\omega_0}{4}. \]

Substituting

\[ h=0.10, \qquad \omega_0=2.0~\text{rad/s}, \]

gives

\[ \lambda = \frac{(0.10)(2.0)}{4}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \lambda = \frac{0.20}{4} = 0.050~\text{s}^{-1}. \]

So,

\[ \boxed{ \lambda\approx0.050~\text{s}^{-1}. } \]

Part 3 — Estimate the Amplitude After 40 Seconds

During exponential growth, the amplitude behaves approximately as

\[ A(t)=A_0e^{\lambda t}. \]

The initial amplitude is

\[ A_0=0.010~\text{m}, \]

and

\[ t=40~\text{s}. \]

Using

\[ \lambda=0.050~\text{s}^{-1}, \]

we get

\[ A(40) = 0.010e^{(0.050)(40)}. \]

Since

\[ (0.050)(40)=2.0, \]

we have

\[ A(40)=0.010e^2. \]

Using

\[ e^2\approx7.39, \]

we obtain

\[ A(40)\approx0.010(7.39)=0.0739~\text{m}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ A(40)\approx7.4\times10^{-2}~\text{m}. } \]

The amplitude has increased from about \(1.0~\text{cm}\) to about \(7.4~\text{cm}\).

Part 4 — Explain the Role of Initial Conditions

The equation

\[ \ddot{x} + \omega_0^2\left(1+h\cos(\Omega t)\right)x = 0 \]

is homogeneous. This means there is no external forcing term independent of \(x\).

Therefore, if

\[ x(0)=0, \qquad \dot{x}(0)=0, \]

then the solution

\[ x(t)=0 \]

satisfies the equation for all time.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Parametric resonance amplifies an existing disturbance; it does not create motion from exact zero.} } \]

In real systems, however, tiny disturbances, noise, or numerical roundoff are usually present, and these can be amplified.

Part 5 — Effect of Damping

Damping removes energy from the oscillator.

If damping is present, the equation becomes something like

\[ \ddot{x} + 2\beta\dot{x} + \omega_0^2\left(1+h\cos(\Omega t)\right)x = 0. \]

Parametric growth occurs only if the modulation supplies energy faster than damping removes it.

If damping is weak, the amplitude may still grow, but more slowly.

If damping is strong, the motion remains bounded and the parametric resonance is suppressed.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Damping introduces a threshold for parametric growth.} } \]

Problem 14 - Parametric Instability: Rolling Motion of a Ship#

A real-life example of parametric instability occurs in ships traveling through waves. Under certain conditions, the waves periodically change the ship’s effective stability. This can cause the rolling motion of the ship to grow rapidly, even if the waves are not directly pushing the ship sideways.

A simplified model for the roll angle \(\theta(t)\) of a ship is

\[ \ddot{\theta} + \omega_0^2\left(1+h\cos(\Omega t)\right)\theta = 0, \]

where

  • \(\theta(t)\) is the roll angle,

  • \(\omega_0\) is the natural roll frequency of the ship,

  • \(h\) measures the strength of the stability variation,

  • \(\Omega\) is the frequency at which the waves modulate the ship’s stability.

Suppose

\[ \omega_0=0.80~\text{rad/s}, \qquad h=0.12. \]

  1. Determine the modulation frequency \(\Omega\) that produces the strongest parametric instability.

  2. Estimate the exponential growth rate using

    \[ \lambda\approx \frac{h\omega_0}{4}. \]

  3. If the initial roll amplitude is

    \[ \theta_0=2.0^\circ, \]

    estimate the roll amplitude after

    \[ t=60~\text{s}, \]

    assuming exponential growth.

  4. Explain why this is a dangerous instability for ships.

  5. Write a Python script that plots \(\theta(t)\) for three cases:

    \[ \Omega=1.5\omega_0, \qquad \Omega=2.0\omega_0, \qquad \Omega=2.5\omega_0. \]

    Use a small initial roll angle and show that the largest growth occurs near \(\Omega=2\omega_0\).

Problem solution

Part 1 — Determine the Instability Condition

The strongest parametric instability occurs when the parameter varies at approximately twice the natural frequency:

\[ \Omega\approx2\omega_0. \]

Since

\[ \omega_0=0.80~\text{rad/s}, \]

we find

\[ \Omega = 2(0.80) = 1.60~\text{rad/s}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \Omega\approx1.60~\text{rad/s}. } \]

Part 2 — Estimate the Growth Rate

The approximate growth rate near exact parametric resonance is

\[ \lambda\approx\frac{h\omega_0}{4}. \]

Substituting

\[ h=0.12, \qquad \omega_0=0.80~\text{rad/s}, \]

gives

\[ \lambda = \frac{(0.12)(0.80)}{4}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \lambda = \frac{0.096}{4} = 0.024~\text{s}^{-1}. \]

So,

\[ \boxed{ \lambda\approx0.024~\text{s}^{-1}. } \]

Part 3 — Estimate the Roll Angle After 60 Seconds

If the amplitude grows exponentially, then

\[ \theta(t)=\theta_0e^{\lambda t}. \]

Using

\[ \theta_0=2.0^\circ, \qquad \lambda=0.024~\text{s}^{-1}, \qquad t=60~\text{s}, \]

we obtain

\[ \theta(60) = 2.0^\circ e^{(0.024)(60)}. \]

The exponent is

\[ (0.024)(60)=1.44. \]

Thus,

\[ \theta(60) = 2.0^\circ e^{1.44}. \]

Since

\[ e^{1.44}\approx4.22, \]

we get

\[ \theta(60) \approx 2.0^\circ(4.22) = 8.44^\circ. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \theta(60)\approx8.4^\circ. } \]

The roll amplitude has grown by more than a factor of four.

Part 4 — Physical Danger

This instability is dangerous because the ship can begin with a small roll angle that appears harmless.

However, if wave conditions periodically change the ship’s stability near the parametric resonance condition,

\[ \Omega\approx2\omega_0, \]

then small rolling motions can grow rapidly.

This is especially dangerous because the growth does not require a direct sideways forcing. Instead, the wave motion changes a property of the system itself. In a real ship, large roll angles can shift cargo, reduce stability, damage equipment, or even contribute to capsizing.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Parametric instability can amplify small roll motions into dangerous large-amplitude motion.} } \]

Problem 15 - Particle Physics: Parametric Particle Production After Inflation#

In some early-universe models, the inflaton field oscillates after inflation ends. These oscillations can periodically change the effective mass of another scalar particle field, allowing particles to be produced through parametric resonance.

A simplified mode equation for one Fourier mode \(\chi_k(t)\) of the particle field is

\[ \ddot{\chi}_k+ \omega_k^2(t)\chi_k=0, \]

where

\[ \omega_k^2(t)=\omega_{k0}^2\left(1+h\cos(\Omega t)\right). \]

Here:

  • \(\chi_k(t)\) represents the amplitude of one particle-field mode,

  • \(\omega_{k0}\) is the natural frequency of that mode,

  • \(h\) measures the strength of the inflaton-induced modulation,

  • \(\Omega\) is the oscillation frequency of the inflaton background.

Suppose

\[ \omega_{k0}=5.0\times10^{12}~\text{s}^{-1}, \qquad h=0.08. \]

  1. Determine the inflaton modulation frequency \(\Omega\) that produces the strongest parametric resonance for this mode.

  2. Estimate the exponential growth rate

    \[ \lambda\approx\frac{h\omega_{k0}}{4}. \]

  3. If the initial mode amplitude is

    \[ \chi_k(0)=10^{-12}, \]

    estimate the amplitude after

    \[ t=2.0\times10^{-12}~\text{s}, \]

    assuming exponential growth.

  4. Explain why this process can be interpreted as particle production.

  5. Write a Python script that plots \(\chi_k(t)\) for

    \[ \Omega=1.8\omega_{k0}, \qquad \Omega=2.0\omega_{k0}, \qquad \Omega=2.2\omega_{k0}. \]
Problem solution

Part 1 — Determine the Resonance Condition

For parametric resonance, the strongest growth occurs when the modulation frequency is approximately twice the natural frequency:

\[ \Omega\approx2\omega_{k0}. \]

Using

\[ \omega_{k0}=5.0\times10^{12}~\text{s}^{-1}, \]

we find

\[ \Omega = 2(5.0\times10^{12}) = 1.0\times10^{13}~\text{s}^{-1}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \Omega\approx1.0\times10^{13}~\text{s}^{-1}. } \]

Part 2 — Estimate the Growth Rate

The approximate parametric growth rate is

\[ \lambda\approx\frac{h\omega_{k0}}{4}. \]

Substituting

\[ h=0.08, \qquad \omega_{k0}=5.0\times10^{12}~\text{s}^{-1}, \]

gives

\[ \lambda = \frac{(0.08)(5.0\times10^{12})}{4}. \]

Thus,

\[ \lambda = 1.0\times10^{11}~\text{s}^{-1}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \lambda\approx1.0\times10^{11}~\text{s}^{-1}. } \]

Part 3 — Estimate the Mode Amplitude

If the mode grows exponentially, then

\[ \chi_k(t)=\chi_k(0)e^{\lambda t}. \]

Using

\[ \chi_k(0)=10^{-12}, \]
\[ \lambda=1.0\times10^{11}~\text{s}^{-1}, \]

and

\[ t=2.0\times10^{-12}~\text{s}, \]

we get

\[ \lambda t = (1.0\times10^{11})(2.0\times10^{-12}) = 0.20. \]

Therefore,

\[ \chi_k(t) = 10^{-12}e^{0.20}. \]

Since

\[ e^{0.20}\approx1.22, \]

we find

\[ \chi_k(t)\approx1.22\times10^{-12}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \chi_k(2.0\times10^{-12}\text{ s}) \approx 1.22\times10^{-12}. } \]

Part 4 — Physical Interpretation

In field theory, each mode \(\chi_k(t)\) behaves mathematically like an oscillator.

If the background inflaton field oscillates, it can periodically change the effective frequency of these modes. When the modulation frequency satisfies

\[ \Omega\approx2\omega_{k0}, \]

certain modes become unstable and grow exponentially.

This growth means that energy is being transferred from the oscillating background field into excitations of the \(\chi\) field.

In quantum language, these excitations are interpreted as particles.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Parametric resonance can amplify field modes and produce particles.} } \]

This mechanism is one reason parametric resonance is important in models of reheating and preheating after inflation.

Problem 16 - Paul Trap: Parametric Stability of a Charged Particle#

A Paul trap confines charged particles using an oscillating electric field. In one direction, the motion of a trapped ion can be modeled approximately by

\[ \ddot{x} + \omega_0^2\left(1+h\cos(\Omega t)\right)x = 0. \]

Here, the oscillating electric field periodically changes the effective restoring force on the particle. This makes the Paul trap an example of a system whose stability is governed by parametric effects.

Suppose a singly charged ion in a plasma trap has

\[ \omega_0=1.5\times10^6~\text{rad/s}, \]

and the modulation strength is

\[ h=0.06. \]

  1. Determine the modulation frequency \(\Omega\) at which the strongest parametric instability would occur.

  2. Estimate the exponential growth rate near this instability using

    \[ \lambda\approx\frac{h\omega_0}{4}. \]

  3. If the initial displacement is

    \[ x_0=1.0~\mu\text{m}, \]

    estimate the displacement amplitude after

    \[ t=20~\mu\text{s}. \]

  4. Explain why Paul traps must operate in stable regions of parameter space rather than inside instability regions.

  5. Write a Python script plotting \(x(t)\) for

    \[ \Omega=1.8\omega_0,\qquad \Omega=2.0\omega_0,\qquad \Omega=2.2\omega_0. \]
Problem solution

Part 1 — Determine the Instability Frequency

The strongest parametric instability occurs near

\[ \Omega\approx2\omega_0. \]

Using

\[ \omega_0=1.5\times10^6~\text{rad/s}, \]

we get

\[ \Omega = 2(1.5\times10^6) = 3.0\times10^6~\text{rad/s}. \]

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \Omega\approx3.0\times10^6~\text{rad/s}. } \]

Part 2 — Estimate the Growth Rate

The approximate growth rate is

\[ \lambda\approx\frac{h\omega_0}{4}. \]

Substituting,

\[ \lambda = \frac{(0.06)(1.5\times10^6)}{4}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \lambda = 2.25\times10^4~\text{s}^{-1}. \]

So,

\[ \boxed{ \lambda\approx2.25\times10^4~\text{s}^{-1}. } \]

Part 3 — Estimate the Displacement After \(20~\mu\text{s}\)

Assuming exponential growth,

\[ x(t)=x_0e^{\lambda t}. \]

The initial displacement is

\[ x_0=1.0~\mu\text{m}, \]

and

\[ t=20~\mu\text{s}=20\times10^{-6}~\text{s}. \]

Then

\[ \lambda t = (2.25\times10^4)(20\times10^{-6}) = 0.45. \]

Therefore,

\[ x(t) = 1.0~\mu\text{m}\,e^{0.45}. \]

Since

\[ e^{0.45}\approx1.57, \]

we find

\[ \boxed{ x(20~\mu\text{s})\approx1.57~\mu\text{m}. } \]

Part 4 — Stability Interpretation

A Paul trap works only when the particle motion remains bounded.

If the trap parameters place the ion inside an unstable region, small displacements grow instead of remaining confined. In that case, the particle can gain energy, move farther from the trap center, and eventually escape the trap.

Thus,

\[ \boxed{ \text{Paul traps rely on stable Mathieu-equation regions to confine particles.} } \]

The same mathematics that predicts parametric resonance also tells us which operating conditions lead to stable confinement.
