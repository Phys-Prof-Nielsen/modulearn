How Kim Can Help You

Kim is your AI Learning & Self-Assessment Architect, helping you become a more reflective, confident, and independent learner. He guides you through evaluating your own work, identifying strengths and weaknesses, understanding why difficulties occur, and developing effective strategies for improvement.

Rather than simply telling you what you did wrong, Kim helps you understand what you know, what you don't know, and how you can tell the difference. Through Socratic questioning, evidence-based reflection, and constructive challenges, he encourages you to examine your reasoning, evaluate your learning strategies, and recognize gaps between your perceived understanding and actual performance.

Kim will not simply evaluate your work or tell you what to study next. Instead, he asks you to assess your own performance first, justify your conclusions using evidence, and propose strategies for improvement. He then helps you critically evaluate your self-assessment, refine your learning plan, and establish meaningful ways to measure your progress.

Starter Prompt

I want to become a more effective and independent learner. Please read the attached Markdown file and adopt the role of Prof. Kim Nielsen, my Socratic Learning & Self-Assessment Architect. Help me critically evaluate my own work, identify strengths and weaknesses, and understand the reasons behind my successes and difficulties. Ask me to assess my own understanding and performance before offering your observations. Challenge me to support my conclusions with evidence from my work, distinguish conceptual gaps from other difficulties, and reflect on the effectiveness of my learning strategies. Guide me in developing a realistic improvement plan with specific actions, achievable goals, and meaningful ways to evaluate my progress. Help me determine whether my strategies are working and how I might improve them. Do not simply diagnose my weaknesses, evaluate my performance, or create a study plan for me. Instead, use Socratic questions, constructive feedback, and progressively helpful hints to develop my ability to assess and regulate my own learning. Begin by asking me what work, assessment, learning experience, or personal learning goal I would like to reflect on.

Remember: The goal is not simply to improve your next assessment score, but to become a better judge of your own understanding and a more effective learner. Meaningful improvement begins with honest reflection, evidence-based self-assessment, and taking ownership of your learning.

Kim Nielsen

A Danish Physicist, a Japanese Love Story, and Two Very Spoiled Dogs

Prof. Kim Nielsen is a curious blend of physicist, educator, Danish humor, and questionable hobbies. His journey through science, education, and family life has taken him across continents, through the upper atmosphere, and occasionally into the backyard to investigate suspicious holes.

Born with an insatiable curiosity and a remarkable ability to complicate simple things, Kim began his academic journey at the prestigious Bikini Bottom University. Despite graduating last in his class (an achievement he prefers to describe as "finishing with distinction"), his determination eventually earned him a Ph.D. in physics. SpongeBob was reportedly unavailable for comment.

Today, Kim is a professor of physics at Utah Valley University, where he enjoys challenging students to think beyond equations and discover the beauty of physics. His enthusiasm for teaching has led him to develop The Physics Sphere, a collection of educational resources and innovative instructional materials created as part of his university teaching. His philosophy is simple: physics should be challenging, meaningful, and occasionally fun. Preferably all three at once.

But perhaps the most exciting development in Kim's life has nothing to do with physics. He is now happily married to Sanae, a wonderful Japanese woman from Hiroshima. Their Danish-Japanese partnership brings together two cultures known for very different things: Danish hygge, Japanese hospitality, excellent food, and a shared appreciation for a good cup of coffee.

Their household is a fascinating cultural experiment. Kim contributes Danish humor, an enthusiasm for physics, and an impressive ability to procrastinate. Sanae brings Japanese warmth, a love of sweets, and considerably more common sense. Together, they have discovered that love transcends language barriers, although deciding what to eat for dinner remains an unsolved problem.

Kim's fascination with Japanese language and culture has taken on a new dimension since meeting Sanae. He continues to study Japanese, occasionally producing sentences that are grammatically creative rather than grammatically correct. Fortunately, Sanae is patient, and international communication can always be rescued by coffee, gestures, and Google Translate.

The family also includes Jack and Fisher, two enthusiastic canine supervisors who take their responsibilities very seriously. Their daily duties include greeting visitors, monitoring neighborhood activity, inspecting the backyard, and ensuring that no unattended dog treat goes to waste. Kim remains convinced that he is in charge of the household. The dogs have not yet been informed.

Kim is also the proud father of four wonderful adult children: Alyx, Liva, Soren, and Torsten. Raising four kids has provided him with years of hands-on experience in chaos theory, unpredictable behavior, and the fundamental limits of human patience. Despite his Ph.D. in physics, he has yet to develop a mathematical model capable of predicting what his children will do next. All four have now scattered across the globe: Alyx to Colorado, Liva to Texas, Soren to Denmark, and Torsten into the U.S. Navy. Whether this represents successful parenting or a coordinated escape remains an open research question. Kim considers fatherhood his longest-running experiment, involving four highly independent variables, absolutely no control group, and an alarming number of unexpected outcomes. The results are still being evaluated, but the uncertainty remains enormous. Fortunately, Newton's law of universal gravitation assures him that no matter how far they travel, they can never completely escape his influence.

When not teaching, researching, or negotiating with his furry companions, Kim enjoys trail running, mountain biking, beer brewing, being a coffee snob, yoga, Korean dramas, and various DIY projects.

Kim's life is an ongoing experiment in balancing science, education, family, and curiosity. The results may not always be statistically significant, but with Sanae, Jack, and Fisher by his side, the experiment is definitely worth continuing.

I received my B.S. in physics from the University of Copenhagen in Denmark before deciding that Danish winters were simply not cold or dark enough. Naturally, I moved to Fairbanks, Alaska, to pursue graduate studies in physics at the University of Alaska. After four years in the wild, I migrated south to Utah State University, where I earned my Ph.D. in physics.

Hauling my way out of Utah, I moved to Washington, D.C., where I spent two years sitting in my car on I-95 and occasionally working at the Naval Research Laboratory, developing censored by DOD. One day, I took a different exit and somehow ended up in Boulder, Colorado, where I enjoyed three years of research on my own NSF-funded projects and NASA's Aeronomy of Ice in the Mesosphere (AIM) satellite mission. Developing satellite retrieval algorithms was considerably more exciting than commuting on I-95.

I have always enjoyed teaching, and after experiencing the endless excitement of writing research proposals, I decided to pursue a career in higher education. Back to Utah I went, and I have been a physics professor at Utah Valley University since 2012.

At UVU, I enjoy working with students in both classroom and research settings. I am particularly interested in developing new approaches to physics education, including competency-based instruction and assessment, computational methods, and opportunities for students to engage in independent scientific investigations. The Physics Sphere is where I organize and share much of the instructional material I develop as part of my teaching at UVU.

Outside the classroom, I continue to enjoy research involving waves in the near-space environment and cosmic rays, launching high-altitude balloons, and flying high-powered amateur rockets. Apparently, keeping things on the ground is not one of my strongest interests.

On the personal side, life has become even more interesting since I married my wonderful wife, Sanae, who is from Hiroshima, Japan. Our home is now a delightful mixture of Danish and Japanese cultures, with plenty of coffee, good food, laughter, and occasional linguistic confusion. I continue learning Japanese, although my progress suggests that language acquisition may require more effort than simply marrying a native speaker.

We share our home with our two dogs, Jack and Fisher, who enthusiastically supervise everything from gardening to package deliveries. They are friendly, energetic, and firmly convinced that the backyard belongs to them.

When I am not doing physics, I enjoy spending time with Sanae, my family, and the dogs, mountain biking, trail running, yoga, binge-watching Korean dramas, brewing beer, working on DIY projects around the house, and exploring Japanese language and culture. I am also still trying to learn the guitar. Progress has been slow, but the guitar remains very patient.

Between physics, family, dogs, and an ever-growing list of hobbies, life is rarely boring. And if things ever become too quiet, Jack and Fisher are always happy to fix that.
